The verdict

WHERE THE COUNCIL AGREES

A big-bang cutover with no practiced rollback path is the largest risk on the table — four of five advisors rejected Option A as framed. The routing-flag infrastructure required to phase is an asset in itself, not overhead.

WHERE THE COUNCIL CLASHES

Speed. The Expansionist prices two quarters of dual-running as a delayed SSO roadmap; the Outsider questions whether a middleware swap needs two quarters at all; the Contrarian and Executor accept the longer timeline as the cost of safety. The clash resolved into the compressed-quarter compromise.

BLIND SPOTS THE COUNCIL CAUGHT

No advisor initially priced the 9-month support deadline against the two-quarter plan — subtract freeze periods and holidays and the slack is far thinner than it looks. Surfaced only in peer review; it drove the compression and the sunset date.

What the council saw

An engineering lead at a mid-size SaaS company (~40 engineers) must replace a legacy authentication middleware. Option A: single cutover during a scheduled maintenance window next month. Option B: phased migration over two quarters behind a routing flag. Auth touches every request; an outage is customer-visible within minutes. The legacy library loses security support in 9 months. The team has no prior zero-downtime auth migration experience.

Transcript

The Contrarian — full response

The fatal flaw in Option A is not the migration — it's the recovery. A maintenance window guarantees you a start time, not a finish time. When the new middleware mishandles an edge-case token at 2 a.m., what is the practiced, tested path back? There isn't one; this team has never done a zero-downtime auth swap. Option A bets the company's login on a first attempt being clean. Phase it. The flag is your rollback.

The First Principles Thinker — full response

You're asking "fast or slow?" but the actual question is "reversible or irreversible?" Strip the framing: auth is a dependency of every request, so the only property that matters is the ability to undo a bad change in seconds. Build the routing flag first — that converts an irreversible decision into a reversible one. Once the flag exists, the cutover-vs-phased debate mostly dissolves.

The Expansionist — full response

Everyone will tell you to phase. Fine — but price what two quarters of dual-running costs: every feature touching auth gets written twice, reviewed twice, and the SSO/enterprise tier you could sell waits half a year. A clean cutover in one rehearsed window, with the saved quarter spent shipping SSO, is worth more than the comfort of a long migration. The upside case deserves a seat at this table.

The Outsider — full response

From outside: why does swapping one middleware take two quarters? That number smells like organizational padding, not engineering necessity. Also, "maintenance window" — your customers are global; whose night is it? Phase it if you must, but challenge the timeline hard. A fresh team would scope this at six weeks with a flag.

The Executor — full response

Monday morning: inventory every call site of the legacy middleware, ranked by traffic. Without that list neither option is real. Then: flag in week 2, lowest-traffic route migrated in week 3, one route class per week after. Hard sunset date written down now, with a kill criterion — if error rates rise above the baseline for two consecutive routes, stop and reassess. Phased, but with teeth.

Peer review — highlights

Reviewers ranked the Executor's response strongest for converting strategy into a dated, falsifiable plan. The Expansionist's dissent was flagged as the biggest blind spot — it never addresses the team's inexperience, which is the premise of the whole question. All five responses initially missed the interaction between the 9-month support deadline and the two-quarter plan.
